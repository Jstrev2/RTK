Most first-time buyers open a spec sheet and start comparing stack heights. That's backwards. Between two sensible trainers, the one that doesn't pinch your toes will beat the one with the better numbers every time, because you'll actually run in it.

Here's how I'd shop, and the three shoes I'd start with.

## Fit decides it, and the specs mostly don't

Nearly every shoe in the beginner tier sits in the same narrow band. The [Ghost 18](/shoes/brooks-ghost-18) has a 36mm stack and a 12mm drop. The [Pegasus 42](/shoes/nike-pegasus-42) is 37mm and 10mm. A two-millimeter stack gap isn't something a new runner will feel, so don't let it make the call.

What you will feel is the first mile. Your toes should have a thumb's width of room in front, your heel shouldn't slip, and nothing should rub. If a shoe feels almost right in the store, it will feel wrong by mile four. Try them on in the afternoon, when your feet are a little bigger, and wear the socks you'll run in.

One more thing about fit: skip the shoe that was great for your friend. Feet differ more than shoe models do.

## The Ghost 18 is the safe first pair

At $150 and 9.8 oz, the Brooks Ghost 18 is the shoe I'd point most new runners to. It's a neutral trainer with moderate cushioning, and our data lists the pros as a dependable ride for every easy run, a fit that suits most feet, and wide sizes. The outsole is durable, so your first pair won't wear out early.

The catch is that it plays it safe. It has little energy return and feels firmer than the max-cushion shoes. If you want something that feels exciting, this isn't it. If you want something you'll forget about while you run, it is.

It's the wrong pick if you know you have a narrow heel that lifts out of most shoes, or if you want a soft, pillowy ride. For that, look below.

## The cheaper way in, and the softer one

If $150 is a stretch, the [Winflo 12](/shoes/nike-winflo-12) runs $110 and weighs 9.7 oz. It's firmer than Nike's premium foams and it's not built for workouts, but it's a comfortable, durable everyday shoe. For a runner doing three easy runs a week, that's the whole job. We cover more of this price tier in [our under-$120 guide](/rundown/good-running-shoes-under-120).

If you want a softer ride, the [Hoka Clifton 11](/shoes/hoka-clifton-11) is $150 with a 42mm stack and an 8mm drop. It's the plush option and it works for walking and standing all day, which suits a lot of beginners. The honest downside is that it's not versatile at faster paces, and the foam is ordinary EVA for the money. Think of it as an easy-day shoe that happens to be a fine first shoe.

## What to ignore on your first pair

Skip the carbon-plated racers. They're built for race day and they cost more than a beginner needs. If you're curious about them later, [how to choose your first racing shoe](/rundown/how-to-choose-your-first-racing-shoe) covers when they make sense.

Skip stability shoes unless a running store or a clinician has a reason to put you in one. Support is a decision, not a default. And don't chase the lightest shoe, because the 9.7 to 9.8 oz range is plenty light for easy miles.

Don't overthink the first month either. A good-fitting neutral trainer will carry you through your first 5K, your first 10K, and the weeks of figuring out what you like. By then you'll have opinions, and your second pair will be a better-informed purchase than your first.

If you'd rather filter by your own foot and budget, the [shoe selector](/tools/shoe-selector) narrows the list in a couple of minutes. Otherwise, try on the Ghost, try on the Clifton, and buy the one your feet forget about.
