Every runner has heard "replace your shoes every 300 to 500 miles." That's a range with a wide gap in the middle, and it's built on average shoes, average runners and average roads. You'll get a better read from the shoe in your hand.

## The mileage range depends on the shoe and on you

The range exists because foam, outsole rubber and body weight all vary. A heavier runner compresses the midsole more each stride. A runner who lands softly on trails wears an outsole differently from someone who scuffs a heel on pavement.

Plenty of daily trainers, like the [Pegasus 42](/shoes/nike-pegasus-42), list durability as a strength in our data, with notes on durable outsoles and suitability for high mileage. Plated race shoes, as a rule, don't last as long. I'd treat 300 as an early check and 500 as the point where you stop asking questions and replace.

The evidence behind the exact numbers is thin. Lab tests show midsole foam loses some of its cushioning over time, but how much that matters to a runner is harder to measure. Nobody can hand you a mileage that's right for your body.

## The signs that beat the odometer

Look at the shoe first. Press your thumb into the midsole. If it feels dead and creased, with deep wrinkles running across the foam, it has lost its bounce. Check the outsole too. When the rubber is worn through to the foam at the heel or ball of the foot, grip and protection are gone.

Then check the upper. A heel counter that has collapsed or a toe box that has torn out lets your foot slide around, and that's worth fixing before the cushioning is.

Now check yourself. If your legs feel more beaten up after easy runs than they used to, and the shoe is past 300 miles, that's a reasonable hint it's time. It isn't proof. A hard training block or poor sleep can do the same. If a pain keeps showing up or gets worse, that's a conversation for a doctor or physical therapist, not a shoe swap.

Here's a simple trick I use. Keep a spare pair that's newer, and do an easy run in each on back-to-back days. If the old pair feels flat by comparison, you have your answer.

## Softer foam usually wears out sooner

Firm shoes feel the same on day 1 and day 300. Soft ones feel different, since the foam is what gives them the ride. That's why the budget [Winflo 12](/shoes/nike-winflo-12), a firmer trainer with a durable outsole, ages gracefully. A plush shoe like the [Hoka Clifton 11](/shoes/hoka-clifton-11) has a 42mm stack of soft EVA and gives you more of a before and after.

Neither is wrong. If you love a bouncy shoe, expect to replace it a little earlier than a firm one.

## Rotating pairs stretches the life of both

Foam needs time to recover between runs. Alternating two pairs lets each one rebound, and in my experience both last longer. A 2015 study followed 264 recreational runners for 22 weeks and found those who used more than one pair had a lower risk of running-related injury. It was observational, so it can't prove the rotation caused it. Still, it's a good reason to try it, and our [guide to rotating two pairs](/rundown/shoe-rotation-two-pairs) shows how.

Log your miles on whatever app you use and set an alert at 300. When it goes off, run the thumb test and check the outsole. If the shoe still passes, keep going.
