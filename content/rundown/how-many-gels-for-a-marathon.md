The honest answer to "how many gels" is a number of grams, not a number of gels. Gels differ, so the useful math starts with carbs per hour and works backward to the packet count.

## Start from carbs per hour, not from the gel

A 2014 review by Asker Jeukendrup in Sports Medicine laid out targets by duration. For events lasting about two to three hours, it recommends around 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour, and for ultra-endurance efforts about 90. The older American College of Sports Medicine guidance was 30 to 60 grams an hour, and a later consensus statement stretched the range to 30 to 90.

Notice that these are guidelines for athletes in general, and the review itself says the numbers depend on the type and duration of activity. For a marathon, 60 grams an hour is a sensible target for most people who can tolerate it. Slower runners who spend four or more hours out there can reasonably aim a bit higher.

The 90 gram figure relies on mixing glucose and fructose, which the body absorbs through different routes. If you're new to fueling, treat 90 as something to build toward, not where you start.

## Then work backward to gels

Most gels carry somewhere around 20 to 25 grams of carbohydrate per packet, and the label tells you exactly. Check yours, because the number varies by brand and by whether it's a thin gel or a thick one. Our own fuel database doesn't have clean carb counts for every product yet, so I won't pretend to quote one.

At 60 grams an hour and 20 grams a gel, you're taking one gel every 20 minutes. Here's what that comes to across finish times:

- A 3:30 marathon takes about 10 gels, with a few fewer if you start fueling at mile 4
- A 4:00 marathon works out to 11 to 12 gels
- A 5:00 marathon runs 14 or so, which is when chews or drink mix start to help with the monotony

Subtract anything you take in sports drink at aid stations, since those carbs count too. If the course drink has 20 grams per bottle and you finish one an hour, you can skip a gel.

## Start early and stay steady

Take your first gel around 30 to 45 minutes in, well before you feel hungry. By the time you feel flat at mile 20, you've waited too long, and I've done exactly that. Small and regular beats big and late.

Water goes with the gel. Take it at an aid station, sip, and wash it down. Gels without water are a common stomach problem, and [race-day fueling mistakes](/rundown/race-day-fueling-mistakes) has the full list of ways this goes sideways.

## Your stomach decides the ceiling

Here's where plans meet the real world. Most runners can build up to 60 grams an hour, but only by practicing it in training. If you've never taken more than one gel on a long run, don't try 11 on race day.

Our guide to [training your gut for race fueling](/rundown/train-your-gut-race-fueling) lays out a progression. The short version is to add a gel to each long run until your whole race plan feels boring.

The [fueling tool](/tools/fueling) turns your finish time and race into a schedule, so you can see the timing before you open a single packet.

This plan is wrong for you if you're running a 10K or a fast half, where under 75 minutes usually needs little beyond water. It's also not a medical plan. If you have diabetes or another health condition that affects how you handle carbohydrates, talk to your doctor or a dietitian before you adopt a fueling target.

Pick a gel you can stomach, write the gel times on your arm or in your watch, and rehearse the whole thing on your next long run.
