You don't need a watch to learn your cadence. You need 30 seconds, a flat stretch of road and a way to count. Once you have the number, it tells you which songs will feel natural and which will fight you.

## Counting steps for 30 seconds gives you the number

Warm up for ten minutes at your normal easy pace. Then pick a stretch where you can run steadily and count how many times your right foot lands in 30 seconds. Multiply by four. That's your steps per minute, which is what everyone means by cadence.

Do it a few times on different days, and at different paces. You'll notice it climbs as you speed up. A runner at an easy pace might sit in the 160s and hit the 170s or 180s on a tempo run. Those are ballpark numbers, not targets, and plenty of good runners fall outside them.

Most running watches report cadence too. If yours does, check it against your hand count once so you know it's reading steps and not strides.

## You almost never need to change it by much

Here's the useful research. A 2011 study from Heiderscheit's lab at the University of Wisconsin put 45 recreational runners on a treadmill at their normal step rate and at plus and minus 5 and 10 percent. Raising step rate by 5 percent cut the energy the knee absorbed, and a 10 percent rise reduced it at the hip too. The authors also noted that a big change from your natural rate may be hard to hold and could cost you speed.

That's one lab study on healthy runners at a fixed speed. It doesn't prove a magic number, and it isn't a reason to chase 180. If your natural cadence is 158, a target of 166 is a reasonable experiment. A target of 180 is a different way of running.

If something hurts when you run, that's a question for a clinician or a physical therapist, not a cadence app.

## Match songs to your cadence, then go easy on the beat

The point of matching music is that a song at your cadence feels like it's running with you. A track at 165 BPM suits a runner at 165 steps a minute. That's why a playlist built for your pace works better than a random shuffle.

The [Music Finder](/tools/music) builds playlists around a BPM or pace you pick, so you can start with your counted number and see what comes up.

Half-time songs are the trick most runners miss. A track at 85 BPM can match 170 steps a minute if you land one foot on every beat and the other between. It means you aren't limited to the fast end of the catalog.

## To nudge your cadence, use songs a few BPM above your own

Pick a song 3 to 5 percent above your natural rate and run to it for a couple of minutes at the end of an easy run. Don't force it for the whole workout. If the beat feels like a leash, drop back to your own rhythm.

Do that once or twice a week and your body will drift toward the new number without you thinking about it. Faster isn't automatically better, and your stride length will shorten a little as cadence rises. That's the trade, and it's the reason I'd keep changes small.
