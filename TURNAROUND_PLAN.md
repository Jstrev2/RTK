# Runner Toolkit: Business, Message, and Site Turnaround

Date: July 21, 2026
Primary objective: turn Runner Toolkit from a collection of running features into a coherent business with a clear audience, an obvious free value proposition, and a paid product people understand.

## 1. The fundamental reset

Runner Toolkit should not be positioned as:

- an all-in-one running app;
- a running magazine;
- a generic training-plan platform;
- an injury-treatment product;
- an AI company;
- a collection of every tool the team has built.

It should be positioned as:

> **The place runners go to make the decisions around their run.**

The three primary free products answer the three most commercially and emotionally useful questions:

1. **What shoes should I run in?**
2. **What should I listen to?**
3. **How should I fuel?**

The paid product answers the higher-stakes question:

4. **What happens to my training plan when something goes wrong?**

That is the business. Everything else must either support one of those four questions or leave the primary experience.

## 2. The business thesis

Runner Toolkit needs a simple value ladder.

### Free value: make the next run easier to plan

The free tools create immediate value without an account:

- Shoe Finder gives a confident shortlist.
- Running Music builds a soundtrack for a specific run.
- Fuel Planner produces a practical schedule and packing list.

These products should feel complete, not like thin lead magnets.

### Commercial value: help runners act on the recommendation

The business earns affiliate revenue when a runner chooses to buy a recommended shoe or fueling product. Commerce belongs at the moment of decision, after Runner Toolkit has earned trust.

### Paid value: keep the training plan useful when reality changes

The premium product should not merely sell “injury recovery.” That need is urgent but episodic, which makes a subscription fragile. The broader recurring job is:

> **Keep my training plan realistic when my body, schedule, or goal changes.**

This includes:

- an injury or pain issue after appropriate safety screening;
- missed training;
- illness and return after illness;
- travel or schedule disruption;
- accumulated fatigue;
- a changed race date;
- a new target time;
- a need to reduce training days.

Injury adaptation can remain the most emotionally powerful use case. It should not be the only reason the subscription continues to exist.

### The business model in one line

> **Free tools build trust and purchase intent; affiliate commerce monetizes gear and fuel decisions; adaptive training monetizes ongoing plan changes.**

## 3. The audience

The current concept speaks broadly to “runners,” which produces generic content. The primary audience should be narrower.

### Primary audience: the committed recreational runner

They:

- run three to five days per week;
- are training for a 5K, 10K, half marathon, or marathon;
- care about improving but do not have a personal coach;
- buy real running shoes and race fuel;
- use Spotify, Strava, Garmin, or similar products;
- have enough experience to know what is frustrating, but not enough confidence to dismiss guidance;
- want a useful answer faster than they can get from reviews, Reddit threads, spreadsheets, and generic training articles.

This audience is valuable because it has all four needs: gear, music, fuel, and training continuity.

### Secondary audiences

- New runners can use the free tools, but the brand should not feel remedial.
- Experienced racers can use detailed filters and comparisons, but the site should not be built only for data obsessives.
- Injured runners are a high-intent paid segment, but they should enter through a dedicated journey rather than define the entire brand.

### The audience is not

- elite athletes looking for a coach replacement;
- people seeking medical diagnosis or treatment;
- general fitness users;
- readers looking for broad running news;
- users who want another manual workout tracker.

## 4. The brand position

### Recommended core line

> **Run smarter. Guess less.**

It connects all four product jobs without forcing them into a false “all-in-one” claim.

### Brand explanation

> Find shoes that fit how you run. Build music for the workout. Plan fuel for the distance. And when training changes, adjust the plan.

### Product promise

> Runner Toolkit gives everyday runners useful, explainable answers—free where it should be, paid where real personalization continues over time.

### Brand personality

- Knowledgeable without pretending to be a coach or clinician.
- Direct without sounding aggressive.
- Energetic without sports-brand clichés.
- Practical rather than inspirational.
- Honest about uncertainty and tradeoffs.
- Built by people who understand the small decisions runners actually make.

### Language to stop using

- “All-in-one running platform”
- “For serious runners”
- “Command center”
- “The only tool you need”
- “AI-powered” as the primary selling point
- “Get you back to the start line” as a guaranteed outcome
- “Prevent injuries”
- “Community-ranked” until there is a real active community
- repeated explanations that the tools are “free forever” on every section

### Writing rule

Every page should answer:

1. What decision is the runner trying to make?
2. What will Runner Toolkit give them?
3. How quickly will they get it?
4. Why should they trust the result?
5. What is the single next action?

## 5. The product hierarchy

The current site treats too many things as products. The new hierarchy must be explicit.

### Tier 1: flagship free products

These define the public brand and dominate the homepage:

1. Shoe Finder
2. Running Music
3. Fuel Planner

### Tier 2: the paid product

4. Adaptive Training

This gets a persistent but secondary navigation CTA. It receives a dedicated sales journey, not the homepage hero.

### Tier 3: supporting utilities

- Pace Calculator
- Free training plans
- Relevant guides
- Saved results/account

These make the flagship products more useful. They do not compete for the brand’s opening message.

### Products to pause or remove from prominence

- Attire Guide
- Manual workout logging
- Generic running news
- Broad music voting/community features
- General-purpose dashboard features
- Any new tool that does not strengthen the four primary decisions

Pausing does not require deleting useful code. It means removing the feature from the main story and stopping investment until the core business works.

## 6. The new homepage

The homepage should work as a product chooser, not a company manifesto or premium sales page.

### Section 1: the hero

**Eyebrow**

Free tools made for runners

**Headline**

> **Run smarter. Guess less.**

**Supporting copy**

> Find shoes that fit how you run. Build music for the workout. Plan fuel for the distance. Get a useful answer in minutes—no account required.

**Primary interface**

Three product starters should appear immediately inside or directly below the hero:

#### Find my shoes

> Tell us how and where you run. Get a short, explainable list of shoes worth trying.

Button: **Find my shoes**

#### Build my run playlist

> Match the music to your workout, duration, pace, and preferred energy.

Button: **Build my playlist**

#### Plan my fuel

> Turn your distance and expected time into a practical eating and drinking schedule.

Button: **Plan my fuel**

The homepage should not default to one hero CTA that makes the other products look secondary.

### Section 2: show the answer, not a feature list

Use one tangible example from each tool:

- A three-shoe shortlist with match reasons and tradeoffs.
- A 60-minute playlist timeline showing warm-up, work, and finish.
- A half-marathon fuel schedule with time, mile, product, and totals.

The purpose is to make the output understandable before the user commits to a quiz.

Suggested heading:

> **Useful enough to change your next run.**

Suggested copy:

> No generic top-ten lists. No unexplained scores. Every result tells you what fits, why it fits, and what to do next.

### Section 3: the paid bridge

This is where Adaptive Training first appears.

**Heading**

> **When the plan stops fitting, change the plan.**

**Copy**

> Missed two weeks? Coming back after an issue? Travel wrecked the schedule? Tell Runner Toolkit what changed and get a revised path to race day—with clear limits when the safe answer is to stop and get professional guidance.

Button: **See Adaptive Training**

Secondary link: **Try a sample adjustment**

Do not make injury imagery or alarming pain language the visual centerpiece. The product is about maintaining a realistic plan, not dramatizing injury.

### Section 4: how recommendations work

**Heading**

> **Recommendations you can understand.**

Three principles:

- **Personalized:** based on the run, runner, and constraints—not a universal ranking.
- **Explainable:** every recommendation includes reasons and tradeoffs.
- **Independent:** affiliate availability does not decide what gets recommended.

Link: **Read our methodology**

### Section 5: contextual guidance

Show a small number of useful guides, organized under the three products:

- Choosing between two similar shoes
- Matching BPM to cadence and workout type
- Practicing a race-fuel plan without upsetting your stomach

Do not call this section “The Rundown.” Call it **Runner Guides** or simply **Guides**.

### Section 6: a relevant email offer

Do not use a generic “Get the drop” newsletter.

Offer preference-based updates:

> **Get updates for the part of running you care about.**

Choices:

- New shoe matches and price changes
- Fuel and race-prep guidance
- New playlists and music tools
- Training-plan updates

The email promise should match the selected interest.

## 8. The flagship product messages

### 8.1 Shoe Finder

#### The job

> Help me narrow a confusing market to a few shoes I can confidently try or buy.

#### Page headline

> **Find the shoes that fit your run.**

#### Supporting copy

> Tell us what you are training for, how you like a shoe to feel, what fits your foot, and what you want to spend. We will give you a short list with reasons—not 200 shoes to research.

#### The questions should emphasize

- What are these shoes for?
- Road, track, treadmill, or light trail?
- How much do you run each week?
- What cushioning feel do you enjoy?
- What width or fit problems do you have?
- What shoe has worked well before?
- What is the budget?

Pronation can remain an optional input, but it should not make the experience feel like a remote gait diagnosis.

#### Result structure

For each recommended shoe:

1. Best-for label
2. Match explanation
3. Tradeoffs
4. Fit and width notes
5. Current model and data freshness
6. Compare action
7. Retailer choices

#### Result language

Use:

- “Strong match for…”
- “Worth considering if…”
- “Probably not your best choice if…”
- “Try this alongside…”

Avoid:

- “Perfect shoe”
- “Prevents injury”
- “Corrects your gait”
- unexplained percentages that imply scientific precision

#### Commercial message

> Compare prices from trusted retailers. Runner Toolkit may earn a commission, but retailer relationships never change the recommendation.

The shopping action appears after the recommendation, not before it.

### 8.2 Running Music

#### The job

> Give me music that fits this specific run and still sounds like me.

#### Page headline

> **Build a soundtrack for the run you are about to do.**

#### Supporting copy

> Choose the workout, duration, music you like, and how you want the energy to change. Runner Toolkit will build the flow from warm-up to final push.

#### The product should be about a run timeline

BPM alone is a filter, not a product. The result should show where songs land during the run:

- warm-up;
- settle-in;
- tempo or interval work;
- hills or hard block;
- final push;
- cool-down.

#### Result structure

1. Total duration
2. Energy curve
3. Ordered track timeline
4. BPM/cadence explanation
5. Replace/reorder controls
6. Preview
7. Save, share, or export

#### Community position

Until real users are creating and saving playlists, do not make votes and inflated scores the primary trust signal. Lead with curated recommendations and clearly labeled popularity data. Introduce community rankings only when they reflect meaningful activity.

#### Role in the business

Music is the repeat-use and sharing product. It may not monetize directly at first. Its job is to make Runner Toolkit enjoyable, memorable, and distributable instead of purely transactional.

### 8.3 Fuel Planner

#### The job

> Turn confusing nutrition advice into a schedule I can carry and practice.

#### Page headline

> **Know what to take—and when to take it.**

#### Supporting copy

> Enter the distance, expected time, conditions, and products you can tolerate. Get a practical fuel and hydration schedule for training or race day.

#### Result structure

1. Simple summary: total carbs, fluid, sodium, and caffeine
2. Time- and mile-based schedule
3. Packing list
4. Assumptions and adjustable ranges
5. Practice version for long runs
6. Product substitutions
7. Buy, print, share, or save

#### Message principles

- Present ranges and assumptions instead of pretending every runner needs the same precise number.
- Separate general guidance from product selection.
- Explain that race-day fuel should be practiced.
- Make caffeine optional and visible.
- Give users a way to replace gels with chews, drink mix, or tolerated alternatives.

#### Commercial message

> Build the plan first. Shop the packing list second.

That sequence protects trust and creates a natural affiliate moment.

### 8.4 Pace Calculator

#### The job

> Give me the pace, finish time, or splits quickly.

Keep it excellent and fast. Do not ask it to carry the brand. Use it inside Fuel and Training so a runner does not repeatedly enter the same information.

### 8.5 Free training plans

#### The job

> Give me a credible starting schedule for my race goal.

Free plans should be clean, printable, and easy to understand. They are not the main differentiator and should not attempt to replace the depth of major coaching apps.

Their strategic role is to introduce the paid promise:

> **A static plan works until life stops being static.**

Every free plan can demonstrate how Adaptive Training changes a week after a missed run, schedule conflict, or other allowable disruption.

## 9. The paid product: Adaptive Training

### The business problem with the current offer

“Pay while injured, cancel when recovered” is understandable but structurally weak:

- the need is temporary;
- the user may be anxious and distrustful;
- the product enters at the moment of highest safety risk;
- an annual subscription makes little sense if injury is the only paid job;
- users may believe they are buying medical recovery guidance rather than training-schedule adaptation.

The solution is not to abandon the injury use case. It is to place it inside a broader training-continuity product.

### Recommended product name

**Runner Toolkit Adaptive Training**

“Plan Rescue” can be used as a campaign or one-time package, but it should not define the whole premium tier.

### Core paid promise

> **A training plan that changes when your training changes.**

### Supporting copy

> Missed time, travel, fatigue, a changed goal, or a medically cleared return to running should not leave you guessing. Adaptive Training rebuilds the remaining schedule, explains the tradeoffs, and keeps the next step realistic.

### What the customer pays for

- a revised schedule, not generic advice;
- continued re-adjustment as circumstances change;
- clear explanations of what changed and why;
- a realistic race-goal update;
- saved plan history;
- calendar/export convenience;
- safety boundaries that refuse inappropriate adaptation.

### What AI does

AI is an implementation detail that helps personalize and explain the plan. It should never be the reason the user is expected to trust it.

The public message is:

> Built from structured training rules, reviewed boundaries, and the details you provide.

The product should clearly distinguish:

- schedule adaptation;
- general education;
- situations requiring a coach or clinician;
- situations where Runner Toolkit will not provide a plan.

### Packaging recommendation

Offer two ways to buy:

#### Adaptive Training membership

For runners who want their entire training cycle to remain adjustable.

Includes schedule, ongoing adjustments, history, and exports.

#### Plan Rescue pass

For a runner facing one immediate disruption who does not want another indefinite subscription.

Includes a fixed access window and a limited number of revisions.

This acknowledges the episodic use case instead of forcing it into a subscription. Annual pricing should appear only when the broader ongoing value is real.

### The sales page story

1. **The problem:** static plans assume perfect weeks.
2. **The consequence:** runners improvise, overcorrect, or abandon the goal.
3. **The product:** rebuild the remaining schedule around what changed.
4. **The proof:** show an original week beside an adjusted week.
5. **The boundaries:** explain what the product can and cannot do.
6. **The offer:** membership or fixed-duration rescue.
7. **The action:** try a sample adjustment before signup.

The page should show the product doing its job. It should not be a long grid of premium feature cards.

## 10. The role of guides and editorial content

Runner Toolkit does not need a standalone publication. It needs content that helps users make the same decisions as the tools.

### New editorial rule

Every guide must do at least one of these:

- help a runner begin a tool;
- help interpret a result;
- answer an objection to a recommendation;
- compare products or approaches;
- teach the runner how to use the result;
- build trust in the methodology;
- create a useful path into Adaptive Training.

If an article does none of those things, it does not belong on Runner Toolkit.

### Content attached to Shoes

- How cushioning feel changes the ride
- Daily trainer versus speed shoe
- How to compare two shoe recommendations
- What width options actually mean
- When to replace a running shoe
- Model-update explainers tied to the catalog

### Content attached to Music

- BPM versus cadence
- Music for intervals versus easy runs
- How to build an energy curve for a race playlist
- Why half-time BPM can still match a run
- Curated workout and distance playlists

### Content attached to Fuel

- How to practice fueling on long runs
- Carbohydrate ranges explained
- Caffeine timing and product comparison
- Gel versus chews versus drink mix
- Packing a half-marathon or marathon fuel plan

### Content attached to Training

- What to do after missing a week
- How to change a plan after moving the race date
- When a pace goal needs to change
- Returning to structured training after appropriate clearance
- How Adaptive Training decides what to reduce first

### Content presentation

Guides appear:

- inside relevant tool introductions;
- beside results;
- on product detail pages;
- inside training plans;
- in a modest Guides index.

They should not interrupt a tool with unrelated news cards.

## 11. The visual concept must serve the message

The new design should make Runner Toolkit feel like an active utility, not a dark dashboard.

### Working direction: The Runner’s Field Kit

- Bright, light-first background.
- Strong black or near-black typography.
- Cobalt for utility and links.
- Safety orange for action and important markers.
- A fresh track or mint accent for progress and positive states.
- Real shoe and fuel imagery.
- Album artwork and timeline visualization for music.
- Diagrams, comparison rails, split marks, and packing lists instead of decorative glass cards.

### Page-level visual identities

- **Shoes:** side-by-side comparison lanes, fit callouts, product photography.
- **Music:** horizontal time track, album art, waveform/rhythm cues.
- **Fuel:** race timeline, mile markers, product icons, packing checklist.
- **Adaptive Training:** original-versus-revised calendar, change annotations, safety boundary callouts.

### Interaction principle

Each flagship page moves through three visible stages:

1. Tell us about the run.
2. See the recommendation.
3. Compare, customize, save, share, buy, or adapt.

The visual system should make the stage obvious. The user should never feel like they are filling out a generic database form.

## 12. Cross-product journeys

Runner Toolkit becomes coherent when products hand off to each other naturally.

### Race-prep journey

```text
Choose a free training plan
→ calculate goal pace
→ build a fuel schedule
→ build a race playlist
→ save the complete race kit
```

### New-shoe journey

```text
Complete Shoe Finder
→ compare three recommendations
→ read one relevant fit/use guide
→ choose a retailer
→ save the preferred shoe to the runner profile
```

### Disrupted-training journey

```text
Open current training plan
→ report what changed
→ see whether Runner Toolkit can adapt it
→ preview the revised week
→ purchase Adaptive Training or a Plan Rescue pass
```

### Reuse of runner information

When a user chooses to create an account, the value should be concrete:

- pace and race goal reused across tools;
- shoe preferences remembered;
- fuel products and tolerances remembered;
- music preferences remembered;
- saved plans and results available across devices.

Do not ask for an account merely to improve signup numbers.

## 13. What changes on the current site

### Homepage

- Replace the injury-led hero with the three-tool promise.
- Remove the six-card tool grid.
- Remove injury language from every section.
- Replace abstract statistics with real result previews and methodology.
- Replace The Rundown with contextual guides.
- Replace the generic newsletter with interest choices.
- Introduce Adaptive Training only after the free products.

### Global navigation and footer

- Reduce seven primary links to four categories plus More and one paid CTA.
- Remove Rundown as a brand.
- Move Pace out of primary navigation.
- Remove the injury slogan from the footer.
- Add methodology and disclosure paths.

### Tool pages

- Give each tool its own message, interaction, output, and visual identity.
- Remove the identical comeback CTA from the bottom of every tool.
- Add only contextually relevant handoffs.
- Show a useful result before asking for an account.

### Training and premium

- Separate free plans from the paid Adaptive Training story.
- Expand adaptation beyond injury-only positioning.
- Demonstrate plan changes visually.
- Offer a sample adjustment.
- Offer membership and fixed-duration rescue packaging.

### Articles

- Stop broad race news and generic running coverage.
- Reclassify useful pieces under product hubs.
- Archive or leave accessible but unpromoted anything that does not support the core decisions.

## 14. Business and content build sequence

### Phase 1: make the strategic decisions

Deliverables:

- final audience definition;
- final brand position and core line;
- approved product hierarchy;
- decision on Adaptive Training scope;
- decision on membership versus Plan Rescue packaging;
- approved list of paused features;
- voice and claims guide.

No page design begins until these are approved.

### Phase 2: write the site before styling it

Create low-fidelity content wireframes for:

1. Homepage
2. Shoe Finder start and results
3. Music start and playlist timeline
4. Fuel start and schedule result
5. Training hub
6. Adaptive Training sales page
7. Sample plan adjustment
8. Methodology page

Test whether runners understand the offer and expected result using plain, mostly unstyled prototypes. The question is not “Do you like it?” It is:

- What do you think this site does?
- Which tool would you use first?
- What result do you expect?
- What would you pay for?
- What makes you trust or distrust it?

### Phase 3: design the product identities

Build the light-first visual system and the four product-specific interaction languages. Design actual outputs before decorative marketing sections.

Priority order:

1. Homepage and navigation
2. Shoe results and comparison
3. Fuel schedule and packing list
4. Music timeline and sharing
5. Adaptive plan comparison
6. Supporting pages

### Phase 4: rebuild the journeys

Ship in business-value order:

1. New homepage and navigation
2. Shoe Finder result and affiliate journey
3. Fuel Planner result and affiliate journey
4. Music timeline and share journey
5. Adaptive Training preview and paid journey
6. Contextual guide system

### Phase 5: launch one coherent business

At launch, every public surface should reinforce the same model:

- three free flagship tools;
- one explainable recommendation philosophy;
- two natural affiliate categories;
- one paid training-continuity product;
- guides that support the tools.

Do not launch with old and new messages mixed together.

## 15. Non-negotiable content principles

1. **One page, one decision.**
2. **Show the result early.**
3. **Answer first, teach second, sell third.**
4. **Do not require an account before value.**
5. **Explain recommendations and tradeoffs.**
6. **Keep affiliate influence separate from recommendations.**
7. **Do not lead with AI.**
8. **Do not imply diagnosis, treatment, or guaranteed recovery.**
9. **Do not publish content merely to appear active.**
10. **Do not allow a supporting feature to compete with a flagship product.**

## 16. Important risks and how the concept addresses them

### Risk: the brand still feels like two unrelated businesses

Response: tie free and paid products to one idea—less guesswork. Free tools solve planned decisions; Adaptive Training solves unplanned changes.

### Risk: music does not monetize

Response: assign music a clear role in repeat use, sharing, preference-building, and brand affection. Do not force an unnatural revenue model onto it.

### Risk: injury-only subscription churns

Response: broaden paid value to training continuity and offer a fixed-duration Plan Rescue option for episodic demand.

### Risk: affiliate revenue damages trust

Response: recommend first, disclose clearly, show multiple retailers, publish methodology, and keep commission out of ranking.

### Risk: the site grows into another feature pile

Response: require every feature to strengthen one of the four core decisions. “Useful for runners” is not enough.

### Risk: guides become a generic content machine again

Response: attach every guide to a tool, result, objection, methodology, or paid journey.

### Risk: AI weakens credibility

Response: sell the quality and realism of the adjusted plan. Use AI only inside structured rules and clearly stated boundaries.

## 17. SEO and technical constraints—not the business strategy

Search should support the new product structure, not dictate it.

While rebuilding:

- preserve useful URLs or redirect them to the equivalent new destination;
- make each tool and useful result understandable without requiring client-side interaction;
- use descriptive product and guide titles based on the real user question;
- connect guides to the tool they support;
- correct the current canonical implementation;
- avoid creating hundreds of thin filter or AI-generated pages.

These are launch-quality requirements. They do not determine the brand, offer, homepage hierarchy, or product roadmap.

## 18. Recommended decisions

Approve these as the default direction:

1. Runner Toolkit owns the four decisions: shoes, music, fuel, and adapting the training plan.
2. The public brand leads with the three free tools.
3. The core message is **Run smarter. Guess less.**
4. Adaptive Training replaces injury-only premium positioning.
5. Injury remains a major use case, but not the global brand identity.
6. Membership and fixed-duration Plan Rescue are both offered.
7. Pace becomes a supporting utility.
8. Rundown disappears as a separate brand; guides live under the relevant products.
9. Attire, manual logging, generic news, and premature community features are paused.
10. The new site is written and wireframed around actual outputs before visual styling begins.

## 19. The final business story

The rebuilt site should communicate this in under ten seconds:

> **Runner Toolkit helps runners make better decisions.**
>
> Find the right shoes. Build the right soundtrack. Plan the right fuel. All free.
>
> When training no longer goes to plan, Adaptive Training helps build a realistic next one.

That is a focused business, a clear homepage, a coherent content system, and a defensible reason for the free and paid products to live under the same name.
